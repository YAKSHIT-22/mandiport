# MSP explained: what it is, and when it applies

The minimum support price (MSP) is the rate at which government agencies are prepared to buy certain crops from farmers. It is announced before the season so that growers know a floor exists before they sow. It is not a price control: private buyers in a mandi are free to pay more or less.

## Who sets it, and how

The Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP) recommends MSPs, and the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs approves them. The recommendation considers the cost of production, demand and supply, price trends in domestic and world markets, and the likely effect on consumers.

MSPs are announced for **22 mandated crops**, plus fair and remunerative prices for sugarcane, and support prices for copra and raw jute. The mandated list covers cereals such as paddy, wheat, maize, jowar, bajra, ragi and barley; pulses such as gram, tur (arhar), moong, urad and masur; oilseeds such as groundnut, mustard, soybean, sunflower, sesamum, nigerseed and safflower; and cotton.

If a crop is not on that list, there is no MSP for it, however widely it is traded. Most vegetables, fruits and spices have none.

## Marketing seasons, and the year labels

MSP is announced per crop season, and the label can confuse:

- **Kharif** crops are sown with the monsoon and harvested from autumn. Their marketing season carries the same year label as the crop year.
- **Rabi** crops are sown in winter and harvested in spring. Their marketing season (RMS) falls in the following calendar year, so the rabi MSP for crop year 2024-25 is the MSP for RMS 2025-26.

Mandiport stores every MSP by marketing season and shows that label on the page, so a wheat MSP shown for 2025-26 is the one that applies to the wheat harvested in the spring of 2026. Both of the government tables we use agree once converted this way.

## What MSP guarantees, and what it does not

MSP is a commitment to buy at that price **at designated procurement centres, for produce of fair average quality, during the procurement window**. That means:

- Procurement is strongest for paddy and wheat, and in states with well-developed procurement systems.
- For pulses and oilseeds, procurement happens through price support schemes when market prices fall below MSP, and is usually capped by quantity.
- Produce must meet quality norms: moisture limits, foreign matter, damaged or shrivelled grain. Failing those can mean rejection or a deduction.
- Registration, land records and a procurement window apply. Missing the window means the floor does not help you.

So a mandi price below MSP is not automatically a policy failure or a chance to sell to the government. It may simply mean procurement has not started, the centre is far, or the lot would not pass quality checks.

## Reading the MSP comparison

The [MSP check tool](/tools/msp-check) shows, for one crop, each state's latest median mandi price against the current MSP, with both dates.

- **Above MSP** usually means private demand is strong, and there is little reason to wait for procurement.
- **Below MSP** is worth investigating rather than acting on immediately: check whether procurement is open in your state, what quality is required, and how far the nearest centre is.
- **Close to MSP** is common in procurement seasons, because government buying supports the market.

Remember that a state median hides variation. One district can be well above MSP while another is below.

## MSP and the price you actually get

Even when you sell at MSP, your realisation differs from the headline:

- Deductions for moisture or foreign matter.
- Transport to the procurement centre.
- Waiting time, which has a cost.
- Payment timing, which can be days or weeks.

Against that, procurement removes price risk on the day and usually avoids commission.

## Where our MSP figures come from

We use two government tables published on data.gov.in: a commodity-wise MSP series for kharif, rabi and other crops, and a rabi series by marketing season. Both are published by the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers Welfare. Where a crop's MSP is listed for several varieties, such as paddy Common and Grade A, we link one variety to the mandi commodity so that a single market price is never compared with two different support prices.

Data lags exist. The latest MSPs published through the open data API may be a season behind the most recent announcement. Where that is the case, Mandiport says which season the figure belongs to, and the [methodology page](/methodology) records the gap. Always confirm the current MSP with your state agriculture department or the procurement agency before you plan around it.
